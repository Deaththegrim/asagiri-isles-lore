Lacquerwork

Lacquerwork in the isles starts with urushi, the raw sap of the lacquer tree, sold in pots on Polishers' Street and dreaded for the rash it raises on careless hands. It is brushed on in thin coats, each left to harden in a damp, dust-free room and then rubbed smooth before the next goes on, so a fine piece takes many coats and many weeks. Once you look, lacquer is everywhere: the black iron plates of Kurogane armour, sword scabbards, hairpins at court, the stand that holds a gambling den's debt book, the case of a physician's kit, the tall hats of licensed onmyoji, theatre masks and the bodies of fighting puppets. Black and red are the common colours, and gold dust sprinkled into wet lacquer marks work made for lords and the court. A lacquered piece outlasts its owner, so households hand down their lacquered trays, bowls and writing cases for generations. A lacquerer's apprentice spends the first years grinding, sieving and suffering the rash before being trusted with a brush.
